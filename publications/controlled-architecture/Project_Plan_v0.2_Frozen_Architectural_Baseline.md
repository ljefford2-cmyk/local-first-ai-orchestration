This document is the controlling corpus-level architecture for the three-volume project.

It supersedes the earlier July 2026 three-paper roadmap as the governing project structure.

Earlier papers remain valid as foundational, supporting, methodological, or illustrative works unless specifically superseded on a technical point.

“Frozen” means the architecture is sufficiently settled to begin Volume 2 engineering work. It does not mean the architecture can never change. Any future change to this baseline must be based on a demonstrated architectural defect, materially new evidence, or an unsupported assumption that affects the governing structure. Implementation difficulty alone does not reopen the architecture.

## 1. Project Purpose

This project develops a three-volume systems architecture for governing increasingly AI-mediated operations in complex organizations.

The central premise is:

**The AI agent is not the system.**

An organization acts through people, professional roles, delegated authority, policies, information, sensors, software, legacy systems, workflows, credentials, controllers, approvals, physical equipment, contractors, external dependencies, records, and professional judgment.

AI models and agents are components within that larger operating environment.

The objective is not to create another AI governance layer.

It is to describe an organizational assurance architecture capable of answering three questions:

1. What is the system that must be governed?

2. How can the organization independently determine whether operations are functioning as intended, what the available evidence permits it to claim, and whether the operating model itself remains appropriate?

3. How can that assurance architecture discover, observe, and interact with the thousands of heterogeneous systems, people, processes, technologies, and physical conditions constituting the real as-operated organization?

The architecture is evolutionary.

Initial architecture diagrams, policies, procedures, configurations, and organizational descriptions are treated as hypotheses about the operating environment.

Historical records, current observation, professional knowledge, physical evidence, and continuing evaluation progressively reveal the as-operated organization.

This is therefore not a set-it-and-forget-it system.

It is a continuously observed, evaluated, governed, and deliberately improved organizational system.

## 2. Governing Three-Volume Architecture

**Volume 1 — Define Reality**

What is the system?

Define the claim-relevant organizational operating environment, its authority structure, and the claims establishing what must be demonstrated.

**Volume 2 — Evaluate Reality**

Is the operation working as intended, what does the evidence permit the organization to claim, and is the operating model itself still appropriate?

Establish independent observation, runtime-control relationships, independent claim evaluation, effect verification, evidence handling, consequence-aware assurance, and governed organizational learning.

**Volume 3 — Connect Reality**

How do we discover and interact with the organization that actually exists?

Connect the assurance architecture to the heterogeneous as-built and as-operated enterprise.

The volumes are analytically separable but operationally interdependent.

Volume 2 specifies required evidence properties.

Volume 3 determines whether the actual enterprise can provide that evidence.

Where adequate evidence cannot be obtained, the system does not manufacture assurance.

The claim must narrow, become indeterminate or unsupported; authority may need to be restricted; or the underlying system must change.

## 4. Volume 1 — System Definition

The organizational operating system is the complete claim-relevant environment through which the organization turns purpose, authority, professional expertise, authorized information, technology, and evidence into consequential action.

The evaluation boundary follows the organizational claim.

A model-only claim may justify a model-only boundary.

An organizational claim concerning safe, lawful, authorized, or effective action requires a boundary encompassing every relevant person, authority, information source, control, execution path, external effect, evidence source, and review function necessary to support that claim.

## 5. Claim Governance

Claim formulation is itself governed.

Whoever defines a claim can influence:

- the evaluation boundary;

- required evidence;

- accepted assumptions;

- detectable failures;

- and what the organization is ultimately permitted to say about its own performance.

Consequential claims must therefore be:

- explicit;

- attributable;

- versioned;

- contestable;

- reviewed;

- and authorized.

A claim record should identify, as applicable:

- claim identity;

- purpose;

- accountable authority;

- scope;

- consequence classification;

- operating assumptions;

- evidence requirements;

- acceptable degraded conditions;

- effective period;

- version;

- status;

- supersession history;

- and retirement status.

Possible lifecycle:

Proposed → Reviewed → Approved → Active → Modified/Suspended → Retired Claim-relative evaluation may not be used to artificially narrow the evaluation boundary until an operation appears successful.

## 6. Consequence Classification Governance

Consequence classification is governed configuration.

It is not merely descriptive metadata.

Classification influences:

- assurance intensity;

- acceptable evidence quality;

- consequence-window requirements;

- human-review requirements;

- degraded-operation authority;

- UI-agent limits;

- effect-verification requirements;

- and scaling of evaluation effort.

Classification criteria, assignments, changes, exceptions, and responsible authorities must therefore be:

- attributable;

- explicit;

- versioned;

- reviewable;

- and supported by evidence appropriate to the claim.

A lower consequence classification may not be used merely to reduce assurance burden.

Loop 2 must evaluate classifications against actual operational outcomes.

Where an operation produces consequences materially exceeding those assumed by its classification, the mismatch becomes a first-class finding requiring review of:

- the classification;

- affected claims;

- evidence requirements;

- assurance strength;

- and related operation classes.

## 7. Authority

Authority may differ in:

- source;

- purpose;

- scope;

- duration;

- delegation;

- revocation;

- expiration;

- exhaustion;

- conditions of use;

- and consequence.

Volumes 2 and 3 consume these definitions.

Authority to act and evidence that action occurred are separate propositions.

Neither proves the validity of the other.

## 8. Volume 2 — Independent Operational Assurance

Volume 2 begins with an approved claim and declared operating model.

It establishes two learning loops without collapsing observation, operational control, and evaluation into one function.

## 9. Loop 1 — Operational Assurance

Loop 1 asks:

Did this operation occur as authorized and intended, did the conditions supporting the claim remain valid, did the intended external effect occur, and what claim does the evidence actually support?

Loop 1 contains three cooperating but distinct functions.

**Independent Observation**

Observation establishes what actually happened.

Observation evidence must not be subject to unilateral suppression, alteration, selective disclosure, or material delay by the component whose behavior the evidence is intended to establish.

**Runtime Control**

Control may pause, restrict, revoke, isolate, redirect, escalate, require authorization, or terminate an operation while consequential intervention remains possible.

The assurance evaluator does not automatically become the controller.

Where the controller influences the claim-relevant outcome, it remains part of the evaluated system.

**Independent Evaluation**

Evaluation determines what the available evidence permits the organization to claim.

Evaluation compares:

Declared Operating Model ↔ As-Operated Evidence and determines whether the applicable organizational claim remains supportable.

## 10. Declared-Model Fidelity

The Declared Baseline is itself a representation.

It does not become authoritative merely because it has been encoded into an evaluation system.

Authoritative organizational sources may include:

- law;

- regulation;

- delegated authority;

- policy;

- engineering requirements;

- operating procedures;

- approved configurations;

- contractual requirements;

- and other controlling organizational artifacts.

Translation of those sources into machine-readable or evaluator-readable operating declarations is a consequential transformation.

The architecture must therefore preserve, as appropriate:

- authoritative source;

- source version;

- translation method;

- responsible person or authority;

- effective period;

- assumptions;

- resulting declaration;

- validation evidence;

- and change history.

Two separate discrepancy classes therefore exist:

Authoritative Source ≠ Declared Baseline and Declared Baseline ≠ As-Operated Reality They are separate findings.

A perfectly functioning evaluator comparing actual operation against an incorrectly translated baseline does not constitute valid assurance.

## 11. Consequence Windows

Preventive assurance is possible only where:

**Detection latency

- Evidence-routing latency

- Decision latency

- Intervention latency** remains less than:

Time to irreversible consequence Two assurance modes therefore exist.

**Preventive Assurance**

Intervention remains possible before the relevant consequence becomes irreversible.

**Post-Consequence Assurance**

Prevention is no longer possible, but reconstruction, evaluation, remediation, accountability, and organizational learning remain available.

**Preventive-Mode Validation**

A Preventive Assurance designation is itself a claim requiring evidence.

It must be supported by measured or otherwise independently defensible evidence showing that intervention can reliably occur inside the applicable consequence window.

Nominal design values are insufficient where real operating latency materially varies.

Relevant latency distributions must be periodically revalidated using as-operated evidence.

Where the evidence no longer supports timely intervention, the applicable assurance claim automatically narrows to Post-Consequence Assurance until preventive capability is re-established.

## 12. Effect Verification

Task completion is not organizational success.

Where the organizational claim depends upon a real-world result, the architecture must independently determine whether the intended effect actually occurred.

Successful execution and successful effect are separate claims.

Effect verification should, where consequence requires it, rely upon a path independently defensible from the execution path.

## 13. Evidence Quality

Evidence may be:

- incomplete;

- delayed;

- unavailable;

- stale;

- contradictory;

- corrupted;

- improperly attributed;

- indirect;

- or potentially compromised.

Evidence attributes may include:

- provenance;

- integrity;

- freshness;

- completeness;

- availability;

- contradiction status;

- time applicability;

- attribution;

- independence;

- directness;

- and known limitations.

Missing evidence is not evidence of conformance.

## 14. Controlling Claim-State Vocabulary

For this three-volume architecture, the controlling claim-state vocabulary is:

**Supported**

Sufficient independently defensible evidence supports the declared claim.

**Narrowed**

Evidence supports a more limited claim than originally declared.

**Indeterminate**

Available evidence is insufficient to determine whether the applicable claim holds.

**Unsupported**

Available evidence does not support the claim.

**Contradicted / Failed**

Available evidence establishes that a required condition or claimed result did not hold.

Pending is a workflow or processing status, not a final claim conclusion.

Earlier papers retaining different terminology remain historically intact. When their concepts are consumed by this project, terminology maps into the controlling vocabulary above.

## 15. Indirect and Degraded Evidence

Indirect evidence is not automatically equivalent to direct evidence.

Its sufficiency is claim-relative and consequence-relative.

Where indirect evidence cannot independently establish the condition required by a consequential claim, it may cap the achievable claim result at:

Narrowed or Indeterminate rather than Supported.

Loss of required evidence may also restrict authority.

For sufficiently consequential operations, degraded evidence may require:

- narrowed authority;

- additional professional review;

- stronger confirmation;

- temporary restriction;

- or termination.

Lower-consequence reversible operations may continue under explicitly governed degraded conditions.

The governing rule is:

Permitted authority and strength of claim must remain consistent with strength of evidence.

## 16. Loop 2 — Organizational Learning

Loop 2 asks:

What do accumulated operational results tell us about whether the declared operating model itself remains appropriate?

Relevant signals include:

- recurring discrepancies;

- near misses;

- overrides;

- false positives;

- false negatives;

- operational drift;

- invalid assumptions;

- obsolete controls;

- incorrect routing;

- incomplete evidence;

- authority conflicts;

- correlated failures;

- unintended incentives;

- environmental change;

- recurring effect failures;

- newly discovered dependencies;

- classification failures;

- and differences between documented and actual practice.

Loop 2 must consider exposure and denominators.

Incident counts alone are insufficient.

## 17. Governed Change

Loop 2 does not authorize autonomous self-modification.

The change cycle is:

Accumulated Evidence→ System Evaluation→ Proposed Change→ Hazard and Impact Analysis→ Controlled Validation or Shadow Operation Where Appropriate→ Accountable Authorization→ Versioned Deployment→ Operational Observation→ Effectiveness Evaluation→ Retain, Revise, Roll Back, or Retire Controls must be removable as well as addable.

The objective is continued fitness, not maximum accumulation of controls.

## 18. Continuous Evolution

There is no final organizational baseline.

People, policy, regulation, equipment, software, AI models, vendors, networks, workflows, environmental conditions, and dependencies continually change.

The lifecycle is therefore continuous:

Discover → Establish Baseline → Observe → Compare → Evaluate → Learn → Authorize Change → Establish New Baseline → Continue Observation The baseline is a versioned, evidence-supported representation of the organization during a particular period.

## 19. Three Baselines

**Declared Baseline**

What the organization says should happen.

**Observed Baseline**

What evidence shows repeatedly does happen.

Observed behavior is descriptive, not automatically legitimate.

**Assured Baseline**

Observed operation evaluated as sufficiently consistent with authorized purpose, authority, controls, evidence requirements, and effects.

The Assured Baseline is not an independent third source of authority.

A material difference between Assured and Declared Baselines must resolve through:

1. an authorized, versioned change to the Declared Baseline; or

2. an explicit, owned, justified, time-bounded exception.

An Assured Baseline may not persist indefinitely as a freestanding shadow operating model.

## 20. Schema and Representation Governance

The architecture’s vocabulary is itself consequential.

Claims, authority, evidence, assumptions, discrepancies, effects, exceptions, outcomes, dependencies, and organizational relationships are represented through schemas.

Schemas determine which conditions the system is capable of expressing and therefore which discrepancies may become visible.

Schema creation and consequential modification must therefore be:

- attributable;

- versioned;

- reviewed;

- tested;

- authorized;

- and recorded.

Schema change follows the governed-change process.

The architecture must periodically ask:

Can the current representation express the failures, relationships, and operating conditions that matter?

## 21. Governance of the Evaluators

The assurance function is itself consequential.

The people and systems operating it can:

- choose evidence;

- define comparison criteria;

- classify findings;

- adjust thresholds;

- shape escalation;

- recommend changes;

- influence leadership attention;

- and materially affect what the organization believes about itself.

They therefore require governance.

A governing body oversees the assurance function.

Its purpose is not to review every operational event.

Its purpose is to determine whether evaluation remains:

- sufficiently independent;

- trustworthy;

- technically competent;

- professionally competent;

- properly bounded;

- adequately resourced;

- and aligned with organizational mission.

The governing body should receive evidence concerning:

- evaluator procedure conformance;

- missing or degraded evidence sources;

- common dependencies;

- false-positive and false-negative performance;

- overrides;

- finding closure quality;

- unevaluated organizational areas;

- threshold changes;

- claim narrowing;

- consequence-classification changes;

- evaluation-model changes;

- schema changes;

- evaluator incentives;

- workload;

- backlog;

- escalation latency;

- repeated findings;

- and effects caused by the assurance system itself.

The evaluators do not govern themselves simply because they are independent of operations.

## 22. Evaluator Capacity

Evaluation capacity is an assurance requirement.

An assurance system that generates more findings than qualified personnel can meaningfully adjudicate is operating outside its own assured envelope.

Relevant health measures may include:

- workload;

- backlog;

- time to disposition;

- escalation latency;

- override frequency;

- repeat findings;

- evidence-package quality;

- reviewer disagreement/divergence;

- consequence-weighted review demand;

- and percentage of operations requiring manual intervention.

The architecture must not preserve the fiction of meaningful human review when practical review capacity has disappeared.

## 23. Bounding Recursive Assurance

The evaluator itself may fail.

The answer is not an infinite hierarchy of equally complex evaluators.

Recursive assurance follows a simplicity gradient.

Higher-order assurance should rely increasingly upon:

- simpler deterministic mechanisms;

- independently protected evidence;

- diversity on critical paths;

- periodic independent review;

- explicit residual assumptions;

- accountable human governance;

- and transparent residual-risk acceptance.

The objective is not absolute independence.

It is sufficient claim-relative independence to preserve a meaningful path capable of challenging or falsifying the claim.

## 24. Common-Mode Assurance Failure

Common-mode analysis must include both operational dependencies and the assurance machinery itself.

Possible common dependencies include:

- identity services;

- network infrastructure;

- time sources;

- cloud control planes;

- certificate systems;

- logging pipelines;

- evidence stores;

- claim registries;

- schema registries;

- comparison engines;

- shared evaluation models;

- policy interpreters;

- and assurance orchestration infrastructure.

For higher-consequence claims, the architecture must determine whether a single defect or authority could simultaneously corrupt:

1. the operation;

2. the evidence of the operation; or

3. the evaluation of that evidence across multiple claims.

Where consequence justifies it, diversity, independent challenge, segmentation, or alternate evidence/evaluation paths should be introduced.

Full redundancy everywhere is neither required nor claimed.

## 25. Claim-Directed and Baseline Observation

Two forms of observation are recognized.

**Claim-Directed Observation**

Evidence specifically required to support or challenge active claims.

**Baseline Operational Observation**

A bounded standing capability intended to identify:

- unexpected dependencies;

- undeclared pathways;

- configuration divergence;

- unexplained authority use;

- emerging operational patterns;

- and conditions not adequately represented by the declared model.

This is not universal surveillance.

Its purpose is to discover evidence that the organizational model itself may be incomplete.

## 26. Volume 2 → Volume 3 Evidence Contract

Volume 2 defines what evidence is required.

Volume 3 determines what the actual enterprise can provide.

Possible capability results include:

**Available as required**

**Available with limitations**

**Available only through indirect evidence**

**Available too late for preventive control**

**Not independently defensible**

**Unavailable**

Unavailable evidence may not be silently converted into assumed evidence.

If the enterprise cannot satisfy an evidence requirement, the claim must narrow, become indeterminate or unsupported; authority may need restriction; or the underlying system must change.

Repeated evidence limitations themselves become Loop 2 evidence.

They may not quietly erode the Declared Baseline.

## 28. Designed, As-Built, and As-Operated

**Designed**

How the system is intended to exist.

**As-Built**

What was actually implemented.

**As-Operated**

How it actually functions over time.

The as-operated environment may contain:

- undocumented dependencies;

- local tools;

- shadow systems;

- human workarounds;

- cross-domain relationships;

- vendor dependencies;

- informal handoffs;

- and evolving practices.

The as-operated enterprise must therefore be progressively discovered through evidence.

## 29. Historical Runtime Evidence

Existing operational records may provide substantial knowledge for bootstrapping the as-operated model.

Possible sources include:

- application logs;

- transaction histories;

- database audit trails;

- network records;

- identity records;

- work orders;

- maintenance records;

- change histories;

- approvals;

- incidents;

- inspections;

- alarms;

- sensor histories;

- equipment records;

- workflow records;

Recorded history is not complete operational history.

## 30. Baseline Bootstrap

A practical discovery sequence may be:

Documented Architecture and Procedures→ Historical Reconstruction→ Passive Current Observation→ Provisional As-Operated Baseline→ Domain and Professional Validation→ Assured Findings→ Authorized Baseline Changes Where practical, consequential automation should not be the first integration mode.

## 31. Graduated Integration

Possible progression:

Observe Only Analyze / Correlate Recommend Prepare Action for Approval Execute Bounded Reversible Actions Execute Higher-Consequence Actions Within Demonstrated Authority Movement between modes is evidence-based and governed.

Greater autonomy is not assumed to be the desired endpoint.

## 32. Claim-Relative Integration

Integration follows the claim:

Claim→ Required Evidence→ Evaluation Boundary→ Required Observation→ Appropriate Interface The architecture does not require one omniscient AI or universal enterprise data lake.

Situational awareness does not require universal access.

## 33. Integration Mechanisms

Possible interfaces include:

- APIs;

- event streams;

- read-only database views;

- telemetry;

- message buses;

- sensors;

- deterministic adapters;

- bounded software agents;

- controlled replication;

- UI/computer-use agents;

- human reporting;

- professional confirmation;

- physical measurements;

- and external evidence.

No interface is preferred merely because it is newer.

## 34. Bounded UI and Computer-Use Agents

Some systems will never expose suitable APIs.

Governed UI agents may therefore be legitimate.

They are not equivalent to native APIs.

Their authority must remain:

- purpose-bound;

- least-privilege;

- attributable;

- revocable;

- observable;

- and consequence-limited.

Where practical, dedicated agent credentials should be used.

For consequential operations, effect verification should preferably occur through a path independent of the UI action.

If no adequate independent effect-verification path exists, the limitation caps the claims and consequence class for which that mechanism may be used.

## 35. Systems That Should Remain Isolated

Not every system should be directly connected.

Some systems may participate through:

- one-way evidence;

- read-only feeds;

- physical measurement;

- professional reporting;

- offline transfer;

- independent reports;

- or deliberately isolated boundaries.

Integration is not the universal objective.

Governed evidence is.

## 36. Continuing Discovery

As-operated discovery never permanently converges.

The as-operated representation is itself an evaluated, versioned artifact.

Volume 3 must account for:

- staleness;

- disappearing evidence sources;

- newly appearing dependencies;

- configuration drift;

- changing interfaces;

- changing work practices;

- and conflicting system maps.

## 37. Workforce and Human-Activity Boundary

Baseline operational observation is directed at organizational operations and system conditions, not generalized employee surveillance.

Evidence collection should be limited to what is necessary for legitimate operational claims and assurance purposes.

Where evidence raises concerns about individual conduct, the matter must route through the organization’s appropriate:

- management;

- professional;

- human-resources;

- labor;

- legal;

- compliance;

- investigative;

- or other authorized process.

The assurance architecture does not independently create personnel judgments merely because operational evidence involves a person.

Volume 3 must treat:

- evidence minimization;

- access control;

- purpose limitation;

- need-to-know;

- employee privacy;

- labor obligations;

- and applicable legal restrictions as integration requirements.

## 38. Situational Awareness

The goal is a connected operating picture, not universal access.

Information must be presented according to:

- role;

- authority;

- mission;

- consequence;

- need-to-know;

- and operational context.

Centralized awareness does not imply centralized authority.

## 39. Scaling Principle

Assurance intensity scales according to factors such as:

- consequence;

- reversibility;

- uncertainty;

- novelty;

- authority;

- evidence quality;

- operational history;

- and demonstrated performance.

Low-consequence reversible operations may receive lighter assurance.

High-consequence, irreversible, novel, poorly observed, or weakly evidenced operations require stronger assurance.

This graded model is itself governed through the consequence-classification requirements above.

## 40. Non-Claims

The architecture does not claim:

- perfect independence;

- elimination of self-certification risk;

- complete enterprise visibility;

- discovery of every unknown hazard;

- prevention of every failure;

- preventive intervention for every operation;

- perfect reconstruction;

- fully automated learning;

- replacement of professional judgment;

- replacement of accountable institutional authority;

- zero residual risk;

- that UI agents equal APIs;

- that every system should be connected;

- that organizational culture is solved by architecture;

- quantified risk reduction without evidence;

- or that this is a complete commercial product.

It seeks to make consequential organizational claims more:

explicit, falsifiable, evidence-supported, bounded, and governable.

## 41. Change-Control Rule for This Project

Future findings must be classified.

**Architecture Defect**

A finding that invalidates or materially contradicts the controlling architecture.

Action: consider amendment of this frozen baseline.

**Volume 2 Engineering Issue**

A problem concerning assurance mechanisms, evidence, evaluation, learning, or evaluator governance.

Action: resolve in Volume 2 without reopening the three-volume architecture unless necessary.

**Volume 3 Integration Issue**

A problem concerning evidence acquisition, interfaces, legacy systems, discovery, scalability, privacy, or as-operated connectivity.

Action: resolve in Volume 3.

**Implementation Constraint**

A practical limitation concerning cost, resources, latency, organizational resistance, technical feasibility, or deployment.

Action: document, test, bound, and design around it. Do not automatically treat it as an architectural defect.

This classification exists to prevent endless redesign whenever implementation becomes difficult.

## 42. Drafting Authorization

The architectural review phase is complete.

Volume 2 drafting is authorized against this frozen baseline.

Future adversarial review should test the implementation of the architecture rather than repeatedly reopening settled premises without new technical evidence.

Volume 2 must remain consistent with this plan while remaining free to discover that specific requirements are impossible, incomplete, or incorrectly formulated.

## 43. Final Governing Principle

The architecture can be reduced to a continuous organizational discipline:

Define what the organization claims.

Establish what authority applies.

Establish what evidence the claim requires.

Verify that the declared model faithfully represents its authoritative sources.

Observe what the organization actually does.

Independently determine what the evidence supports.

Intervene before consequence where demonstrated capability genuinely permits it.

Verify the external effect.

Learn from differences between intended and actual operation.

Govern consequence classifications, schemas, evaluators, and the assurance machinery itself.

Authorize changes deliberately.

Continuously rediscover the organization as it evolves.

The objective is not an autonomous organization.

The objective is an organization capable of seeing itself more accurately, acting within valid authority, challenging its assumptions, learning from operational evidence, and improving without surrendering control to any single person, department, vendor, model, agent, controller, or evaluation system.

Define reality.Evaluate reality.Connect reality.Govern the learning.
